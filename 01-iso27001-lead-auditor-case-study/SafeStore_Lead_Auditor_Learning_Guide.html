    The big picture in one minute

    A company called SafeStore wants a certificate saying its ISMS meets the ISO/IEC 27001 standard. An independent certification body sends three auditors to check. You play the Lead Auditor. The assessment has five parts that follow the real life cycle of an audit:

    1. Plan→2. Lead the team→3. Conduct→4. Report & close→5. Professional behaviour

    Each part has an Excel workbook with pale-yellow cells to fill in. The guiding rulebook for how to audit is ISO 19011. The rulebook for what is checked is ISO/IEC 27001.

    Learn: two standards, two jobs ISO/IEC 27001:2022 says what a good information-security management system must contain. It is the audit criteria. ISO 19011 says how to run any management-system audit well: planning, interviewing, sampling, reporting. Think of 27001 as the exam syllabus and 19011 as the examiner's handbook.

    My overall method (copy this)

    1. Read the brief first (Assessment.txt and the Part text file). Write down exactly what must be produced.
    2. Open the workbook and map every tab. Find the yellow cells, the drop-down lists and the hidden _Lists tab that holds the drop-down options. These options often hint at the expected answer.
    3. Read every SafeStore document and take notes on facts, dates, risk ratings, people and inconsistencies.
    4. Answer using evidence – every statement should trace back to a document.
    5. Check consistency across parts. Part Three findings must match the Part One plan, Part Four must match Part Three, and so on.
    6. Check your work: no empty yellow cells, text readable, drop-downs still working.
    Chapter 1 · Foundations

    ISO/IEC 27001 in plain English

    Information has three properties worth protecting, called the CIA triad: Confidentiality (only the right people see it), Integrity (it stays correct and complete) and Availability (it is there when needed). An ISMS is the set of policies, people, processes and technology an organisation uses to manage those risks in an organised, repeatable way.

    The structure of ISO/IEC 27001:2022

    Clauses 0–3 are introduction, scope, references and terms – they are not audited. Clauses 4–10 are mandatory requirements: an organisation cannot exclude any of them.

    ClauseNamePlain meaningSafeStore example
    4Context of the organisationUnderstand your business, the interested parties, and decide the ISMS scope.Scope Statement v1.0
    5LeadershipTop management commits, sets the policy and assigns roles.ISMS Policy approved by the Managing Director; Roles document
    6PlanningAssess and treat risks (6.1), set security objectives (6.2), plan changes (6.3).Risk Assessment & Treatment Plan, SoA, Objectives document
    7SupportResources, competence, awareness, communication, documented information.Training records, document control
    8OperationActually run the controls and carry out the risk treatment.Backups running, access reviews happening
    9Performance evaluationMonitor and measure, internal audit, management review.Internal audit Nov 2024; management review Jan 2025
    10ImprovementFix nonconformities with corrective action, and keep improving.Corrective Action Register

    A handy memory trick: clauses 4–10 follow Plan–Do–Check–Act. Clauses 4–7 are Plan, 8 is Do, 9 is Check and 10 is Act.

    Annex A – the control menu

    Annex A lists 93 controls in four themes. An organisation picks the ones it needs, based on its risks.

    Trap: control numbers matterMany controls were renumbered between the 2013 and 2022 editions. The deficient audit plan in this case had three wrong numbers, and spotting them was part of Part One. Always check numbers against the organisation's Statement of Applicability or the standard itself.

    Risk: the engine of the ISMS

    ISO 27001 is risk-based: you choose controls because of the risks you found. SafeStore's method:

    • Likelihood (1–5) × Consequence (1–5) = risk score (1–25).
    • 1–4 Low · 5–9 Moderate · 10–16 High · 17–25 Extreme.
    • Inherent risk is the risk before controls. Residual risk is the risk after existing controls. Target risk is the risk after planned treatment.
    • Each risk has a risk owner, a treatment decision (treat, accept, share, avoid), and linked Annex A controls.
    Learn: the Statement of Applicability (SoA)The SoA (required by clause 6.1.3) lists all 93 Annex A controls. For each it says whether the control applies, why, and whether it is implemented. It is the bridge between the risk register and the controls. Auditors always check that the SoA and the risk register agree.

    How certification works

    Stage 1 – readiness & documents→Stage 2 – implementation & effectiveness→CB decision→Surveillance audits (yearly)→Recertification (every 3 years)

    Stage 1 checks that the ISMS is designed and documented and that the organisation is ready. Stage 2 – our audit – checks that the ISMS is actually working: are people following the documents, and do the controls achieve their purpose? The audit team only recommends. The certification body makes the certification decision. The rules for certification bodies are in ISO/IEC 17021-1, with ISMS-specific rules in ISO/IEC 27006.

    The vocabulary of every audit

    Objectives

    What the audit is trying to achieve. Example: "determine conformity of the ISMS with ISO/IEC 27001 clauses 4–10".

    Scope

    The extent and boundaries: which locations, processes, systems and time period.

    Criteria

    What you compare evidence against: the standard, the organisation's policies, legal requirements.

    Evidence → Finding → Conclusion

    You collect evidence, compare it with the criteria to get a finding, and combine findings into a conclusion.
    Trap: objectives vs methods"Review documentation" is not an objective. It is a method (how you work). An objective is the result you are after. This was one of the errors in the deficient plan.

    Types of finding

    This case uses the certification body's own criteria (Certification_Body_Criteria.pdf):

    TypeMeaningExample in this case
    ConformityEvidence shows the requirement is met.C1 access restriction; C2 backup
    Minor NCA limited or isolated failure, not systemic, and no significant doubt about the ISMS.N1 secure disposal
    Major NCA significant or systemic failure that creates substantial doubt, e.g. a required process missing completely or many related failures.None found
    OFIOpportunity for improvement: no requirement is broken, but things could be better.Supplier review schedule; training record dating
    Insufficient evidenceNot enough reliable evidence to decide either way – verify more first.The undated training screenshot alone

    Good evidence: relevant, reliable, sufficient

    • Relevant – it actually relates to the criterion you are testing.
    • Reliable – you can trust it. A system record you watched being generated is more reliable than an undated screenshot someone hands you.
    • Sufficient – there is enough of it, from enough sources, to support a conclusion.
    Learn: triangulationStrong auditors confirm a fact from three angles: what people say (interview), what is written (documents and records), and what is seen (observation or a live system). In this audit, the Operations Manager said the disposal procedure was not approved, the procedure was in draft, and the three devices were seen in storage. Three angles, one conclusion.

    Sampling

    You cannot check everything in one day, so you take a sample from a population. ISO 19011 describes two kinds:

    • Judgement-based sampling – the auditor chooses items using knowledge and risk. Best for small organisations and short audits.
    • Statistical sampling – random selection with a mathematically set size. Needs large populations and more time.

    Either way there is sampling risk: your sample might not represent the whole population. You reduce it by choosing samples yourself (not letting the auditee pick), weighting them to risk, and expanding the sample when you find a problem.

    Meet SafeStore Pty Ltd

    SafeStore is a small Melbourne company that provides encrypted cloud storage to individuals and small businesses through a web portal and mobile app. All data is hosted in Australian data centres run by third parties.

    Chapter 5 · Practical skill

    How to read and fill the workbooks

    1. Find the input cells. Only pale-yellow cells are yours. Grey cells are labels – never change them.
    2. Unhide _Lists. Right-click any tab → Unhide → _Lists. This hidden tab holds the drop-down options. For example, it contains "0 major, 1 minor (A.7.14), 2 OFIs" and "Recommend certification subject to corrective action". These options reveal what the course designer expects, so use them to check your own reasoning (not to replace it).
    3. Read the labels carefully. Some rows are explicitly linked to ISO 19011 clauses (e.g. "clause 6.3.2.2"). That tells you which part of the standard to apply.
    4. Drop-down cells: pick the closest option. If the explanation matters, I picked the list value and added a short note after it (e.g. "Incomplete – the plan has no…"). Excel only checks drop-downs when you type, so this works – but if your marker wants only the list value, keep the cell to the value alone.
    5. Keep the files safe. I saved completed copies (…_COMPLETED.xlsx) and never overwrote the templates.
    6. Check readability. Make rows tall enough for wrapped text, and widen any column that clips your answer.
    Learn: data validationA drop-down in Excel is called data validation. If you edit these workbooks with some programming libraries (for example Python's openpyxl), the drop-downs can be silently deleted. I edited the file's internal XML directly to keep them. If you fill the workbooks by hand in Excel, you don't need to worry about this.
    Part One · Workbook Stage 1

    Plan the audit

    The task: evaluate a deficient draft audit plan (tab 1A), then write a corrected Final Audit Plan (tab 1B). Rule: don't record findings while planning.

    Step 1 – Build your mental model before judging the plan

    I read the scope, risk register, SoA, legal register, policies and the internal audit/management review summary first. Only then did I open the draft plan. You can't spot what's wrong until you know what right looks like.

    Step 2 – Feasibility (tab 1A, section 1)

    Learn: audit feasibilityFeasibility asks: can this audit achieve its objectives with the time, people, information and access available? ISO 19011 considers this before the audit starts (the workbook cites clause 6.2.3). If something isn't feasible, you propose a practical alternative rather than giving up.

    My answer: "Yes" – but only if the plan is corrected. The draft plan as written was not feasible:

    • It included the third-party data centres in scope, but SafeStore excluded them and the team has no right to enter.
    • The Technical Auditor had to cover access, backup, encryption, network security, vulnerability management and software development in one day.
    • Access and backup (the two highest-rated risks) shared one hour, and A.7.14 had no time at all.
    • The Operations Manager holds evidence for nearly everything, so double-booking would stall several areas.
    How I got the practical alternatives

    For each "can't do", I asked: what other evidence would answer the same question?

    • Can't visit data centres? Audit how SafeStore manages them instead: contracts, data-location clauses and supplier assurance reports (ISO 27001 certificate, SOC 2) under A.5.19–A.5.23.
    • No live system access? SafeStore drives a read-only screen share while you direct the queries, or exports reports while you watch, with dates and system details visible.
    • Person unavailable? Use a named deputy, or a remote interview later the same day.
    • Time running short? Re-prioritise by risk and report what wasn't covered as a limitation.

    Sampling, risks, communication, confidentiality, reporting

    • Sampling: judgement-based (the drop-down option), selected by the auditors from complete lists, with 100% checks for tiny populations such as the three retired devices. Expand the sample when a problem appears.

    • Reporting: the Lead Auditor writes the report, issued within 10 working days, and the CB decides.
    My mistake – and the lessonNo audit date was given, so I first chose December 2026 (close to today's date). Later I noticed that every SafeStore document lives in 2025: the management review in January 2025, actions due 31 March 2025, the next internal audit in Q4 2025. A 2026 date would make every annual review look overdue and create findings the case never intended. I changed it to 10 September 2025. Lesson: always check your planning assumptions against the dates in the documents.

    The opening meeting (tab 2A)

    The workbook asks for the three purposes (the workbook cites ISO 19011 clause 6.4.3):

    1. Confirm that all participants agree to the audit plan.
    2. Introduce the audit team and their roles.
    3. Make sure all planned activities can be performed.

    Each checklist item was marked with a drop-down value (Confirmed, Explained, Agreed, Confirmed available), plus notes. I included realistic questions from SafeStore. For example, the CTO asked whether the data centres would be inspected. The answer was no – they are excluded – but supplier management would be checked instead.

    Monitoring the audit and managing change

    At each checkpoint the Lead Auditor compares progress with the plan, checks whether evidence is relevant, reliable and sufficient, spots delays, and adjusts timing, samples, methods or assignments. Every change is agreed with the auditee contact, recorded in the audit log, and mentioned in the report if it affects coverage.

    Situation 1 – Threat to impartiality

    An auditor recently worked at SafeStore, in the very area they were given.

    How I answered (and why)
    • Threat: a self-review and familiarity threat – they would be judging their own past work and former colleagues.
    • Immediate action (drop-down: "Move the auditor to a different area"): stop their work in that area at once and contact the CB.
    • Safeguard: reassign the area. If the CB allows them to stay, give them unrelated work and review all of it. If the CB's cooling-off rule applies (commonly two years), withdraw them from the audit.
    • Completed work (drop-down: "Re-check anything that auditor already looked at"): nothing they gathered is used until it has been independently re-verified.
    • Informed: audit client and Lead Auditor, plus the CB. Recorded in the audit log, the CB impartiality register and the report.

    Situation 2 – Evidence unavailable

    I chose a realistic case from the evidence pack: the training dashboard screenshot had no date and no underlying records, and the individual records couldn't be produced in time.

    How I answered
    • Effect: awareness under clause 7.2/7.3 can't be confirmed either way. The rest of the audit continues.
    • Alternatives: interview staff, check signed induction forms, ask the Support Lead to show their own record live.
    • Change: move the check to the follow-up block, agree it with the CTO, and log it.
    • Limitation (drop-down: "Write a clear limit rather than assume it is fine"): state it in the report. Never treat missing evidence as conformity.

    Reviewing draft findings (tab 2C)

    For each draft finding: confirm the criterion → judge the evidence → decide whether more checking is needed → check the wording and grade → retain, revise, reclassify or withdraw → record why.

    Draft findingDecisionReasoning
    F1 – A.7.14 minor NCReviseThe grade stays minor, but the wording "no disposal records" overstates the problem: no device has been disposed of yet. The real gap is that there is no approved method to verify devices are wiped before disposal or re-use, and the R4 treatment is overdue.
    F2 – Supplier OFIRetainReviews do happen at contract renewal, and ISO 27001 doesn't demand a documented schedule – so no requirement is broken. It would be reclassified to a minor NC if a critical supplier turned out never to have been reviewed.
    Learn: diverging opinionsA diverging opinion is a disagreement that can't be resolved. Inside the team, go back to the criterion and the evidence; the Lead Auditor decides and records the reasons. With the auditee, listen, accept new evidence, and revise if it is justified. If it is still unresolved, record both positions in the report and explain the CB's appeals process. Never say "the auditor's decision is final."
    Part Three · Workbook Stage 3

    Conduct the audit

    The task: build the checklist and sampling record (3A), write findings (3B), run the debrief (3C), and review the ISMS documents and SoA (extra tab). You need at least one conformity and one nonconformity.

    Interviewing like an auditor

    • Open questions: "Describe how…", "Show me…", "How do you know…?" – not "Do you review access?" (a yes/no question teaches you nothing).
    • Follow up: "Can I see the last one?", "What happens when it fails?"
    • Verify: every important statement is checked against a record or the live system.
    • Chase inconsistencies. The 2024 internal audit mentioned annual access reviews, but the CTO said quarterly. I asked for the documented review schedule (created as a 2024 corrective action), which confirmed quarterly. That resolved it.

    Writing findings (tab 3B, "A.18 format")

    The workbook calls this the "A.18 format". A conformity records the criterion, the evidence and a declaration. A nonconformity records the criterion, the evidence, why the criterion is not met, related findings and the grade.

    C1 A.8.3 Information access restriction – Conformity

    Evidence: the policy requires least privilege, just-in-time access, MFA and expiry dates. The CTO and Developer confirmed this. The sample of three accounts matched their roles, MFA was on, expiry was set on the temporary account, and all three were in the latest review.

    C2 A.8.13 Information backup – Conformity

    Evidence: the procedure requires daily backups, encryption, an offsite copy and quarterly restore tests. The logs were successful and encrypted, the month-end backup had an offsite copy, and the restore test was successful – meeting the 100% restore objective.

    N1 A.7.14 Secure disposal or re-use – Minor nonconformity

    Evidence: the Ops Manager's interview, an equipment register showing 3 devices awaiting disposal, the procedure still in draft, no erasure method or records, the SoA status "not fully implemented", R4 effectiveness "Poor", and the treatment due 31 Mar 2025 not done.

    Why the requirement isn't met: A.7.14 expects equipment with storage media to be checked so that sensitive data is removed or securely overwritten before disposal or re-use. SafeStore has no approved method, responsibilities or evidence requirements for that check.

    Learn: how to grade major vs minor (my decision steps)
    1. Is a requirement actually broken? No → OFI or conformity. Yes → continue.
    2. Is it systemic – many related failures, or a required process missing or completely broken? Yes → major.
    3. Does it create substantial doubt that the ISMS achieves its outcomes? Yes → major.
    4. Otherwise → minor.
    For N1: it is a single control, the devices are locked away, none has been disposed of, and no data was exposed. Access, backup and the rest of the ISMS work. So it is minor.
    Trap: "missing procedure = major"?It's tempting to say "the procedure is missing, so a required process is absent → major". But ISO 27001 doesn't require a written disposal procedure as such. It requires the control outcome. SafeStore has compensating measures (restricted access, records retained), so it isn't a complete breakdown. Always grade on the extent and significance shown by the evidence.

    Opportunities for improvement

    • OFI1 A.5.19–A.5.22: suppliers are reviewed only at renewal, with no documented periodic schedule.
    • OFI2 A.6.3: training reporting doesn't show its date or link to individual records.

    Notice the wording: an OFI says what could be better, not how to fix it. That keeps the auditor impartial (see consultancy).

Proposed result: 0 major, 1 minor (A.7.14), 2 OFIs (a drop-down option). I also recorded positive practices, such as automatic expiry of elevated access and closed internal-audit nonconformities.

    Part Four · Workbook Stage 4

    Report and close the audit

    The closing meeting (tab 4A)

    The closing meeting presents the findings and conclusions so SafeStore understands them, and explains what happens next. What I recorded:

    • Attendees: the audit team, MD, CTO, Ops Manager, Support Lead and Developer.
    • Findings: "1 minor NC (A.7.14) and 2 OFIs presented with evidence".
    • Conclusion: "ISMS is effective overall with one minor gap".
    • Recommendation: "Recommend certification subject to corrective action".
    • Deadlines: a corrective action plan within 30 days. The CB must accept it before deciding.
    • Sampling limitations stated, questions answered, no revised findings, no unresolved disagreements.
    Learn: recommendation vs consultingAuditors may say what the gap or improvement area is ("no documented schedule for supplier reviews"). They must not say how to fix it ("use this tool, write this procedure"). Advising on fixes is consultancy: the auditor would later be auditing their own advice, which threatens impartiality.

    The final audit report (tab 4B)

    INCLUDE

    Purpose; objectives, scope, criteria; date, location, client, team, participants; methods, sampling, limitations; findings with evidence and grades; conclusion; recommendation; unresolved disagreements; follow-up requirements; distribution and confidentiality.

    EXCLUDE

    Unsupported opinions; consulting advice; unnecessary confidential data (customer data, credentials, detailed security settings); any claim that the audit team grants the certificate.

    The findings table counts 0 major / 1 minor / 2 OFIs. The report is issued within 10 working days through the CB's secure portal to the MD, CTO and CB only.
